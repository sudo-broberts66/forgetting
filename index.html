One question · three experiments on yourself

Why do we forget things almost instantly after we hear them?

Often because they were never stored in the first place. Speech arrives once and is gone. What you hear lingers for a few seconds as a kind of echo, then has to fit into a mental workspace that holds only a handful of items at a time. Unless you attend to it, repeat it, or connect it to something you already know, it is no longer there when you reach for it.

Test it on yourself Skip to the explanation Three short demos, about 5 minutes in total.
Part 1

Watch your own memory fail, on purpose

The claims on this page come from published experiments, and the simplest of them need nothing more than a screen and a keyboard. These three are simplified versions of classic tasks. They are demonstrations, not tests: one person, a few trials, no controls. Nothing you type leaves your browser.

Lab 1 about 2 minutes · 6 rounds

Hold three letters for a few seconds

Three letters is far less than anyone's memory can hold. The catch is what you have to do while holding them.

  1. Three letters appear for 2 seconds. Remember them.
  2. A number appears. Count backward from it by threes, typing each answer (487 → 484 → 481 …) as fast as you can.
  3. When the bar runs out, type the three letters.

Lab 2 about 2 minutes

How many digits fit?

Digits arrive one per second. When they stop, type them back in order. The list grows by one each time you get it right, and the demo ends after two misses at the same length.

Lab 3 about 1 minute · 2 rounds

Twelve letters, twice

A row of twelve letters appears for 6 seconds. When it disappears, type as many as you can, in order. Then do it once more with a different row.

Part 2

Four places a spoken sentence can get lost

A useful first map, going back to Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968), treats memory as a series of stages. Information is lost at every one of them, and most of it never reaches the last. Select a stage to see what happens there and what the evidence is.

Schematic only. The narrowing band shows the direction of loss, not measured amounts.

The stage picture is a simplification. Later accounts replace the single short-term store with a multi-part working memory (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974) or treat it as the currently active, attended part of long-term memory (Cowan, 2001). The limits described here show up under all of these accounts.

Part 3

Does it fade, or does it get pushed out?

Everyone agrees that unrehearsed information becomes unavailable within seconds. Researchers still disagree about why, and a fair answer to the question on this page has to say so.

Time decay

  • Brown (1958) proposed that a memory trace simply weakens with time unless it is rehearsed.
  • Barrouillet, Bernardin & Camos (2004) keep a role for time: in their model traces decay while attention is busy elsewhere and are restored when attention returns to them.

Traffic interference

  • Keppel & Underwood (1962) found almost no forgetting on the very first trial of the three-letter task. Forgetting appeared as earlier trials piled up and got in the way.
  • Waugh & Norman (1965) varied presentation speed and found recall depended mainly on how many items came after the target, not on how much time had passed.
  • Lewandowsky, Oberauer & Brown (2009) reviewed the evidence and argued that time-based decay is not needed to explain verbal short-term forgetting.

Where this stands. The field has not converged. A 2018 review set out a list of benchmark findings meant as common ground for competing theories of short-term memory (Oberauer et al., 2018). For everyday purposes the two views predict the same thing: if more speech keeps arriving and you do nothing with what you just heard, expect to lose it.

Run Lab 1 and your own first round versus later rounds will show up here.

Part 4

Myth check

Four things you may have been told about memory. Decide what you think, then check.

We remember 10% of what we read and 20% of what we hear.
No evidence behind it

These tidy percentages are usually attributed to Edgar Dale's "Cone of Experience". Dale's cone contained no numbers, and a detailed search for the origin of the figures found no research that supports them (Subramony et al., 2014). How much of a talk you retain depends on what you do with it, not on the fact that it reached you through your ears.

Short-term memory holds seven things.
Outdated shorthand

Miller (1956) noted that people can repeat back about seven digits, and in the same paper stressed that the unit is the chunk, not the item. When rehearsal and grouping are prevented, the estimate drops to about four chunks, with a typical range of three to five (Cowan, 2001).

The forgetting curve proves you lose half of a lecture within an hour.
Over-reading one dataset

The curve comes from Ebbinghaus (1885), who tested one person (himself) on lists of meaningless syllables. He did not measure the percentage recalled. He measured savings: how much less time it took to relearn a list than to learn it the first time.

A modern replication with one participant found a similar curve (Murre & Dros, 2015), so the shape is real for that kind of material. It does not tell you what share of a meaningful lecture you will remember, and it describes forgetting over hours and days, not the seconds-long loss this page is about.

Forgetting something right away means my memory is bad.
Not supported

The limits in Part 2 were measured in ordinary, healthy adults, many of them university students. Losing unrehearsed details within seconds is the normal result. Some researchers go further and argue that losing detail is part of what memory is for, because it favours flexible decisions over a perfect record (Richards & Frankland, 2017). That is a theoretical argument, not a settled finding. This page cannot assess anyone's memory; if yours has changed in a way that worries you, that is a question for a clinician.

Part 5

What helps, according to the evidence

Each of these targets one of the four places where information is lost.

  1. Give it your attention at the moment it arrives

    Doing a second task while taking information in sharply reduced later memory in Craik et al. (1996). Attention that goes to a phone, or to planning what you will say next, is not available for encoding.

  2. Do something with the meaning

    Words that people had judged for meaning (does it fit this sentence?) were later remembered far better than words they had judged for typeface or sound (Craik & Tulving, 1975). Ask what a new fact implies, or how it connects to something you already know.

  3. Chunk it

    Grouping items into familiar units lets the same limited workspace carry more (Miller, 1956). With more than 230 hours of practice at recoding digits into meaningful groups, one student raised his digit span from 7 to 79 (Ericsson, Chase & Faloon, 1980).

  4. Say it back, then quiz yourself

    Repeating something immediately keeps it active. Recalling it later does more: students who practised retrieving a passage remembered more of it two days and one week later than students who reread it, even though rereading looked better after five minutes (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).

  5. Come back to it later

    Spreading study over time beats cramming the same amount into one session across a large body of experiments (Cepeda et al., 2006). A review of ten study techniques rated practice testing and distributed practice as the two with high utility, and rereading and highlighting as low (Dunlosky et al., 2013).

Limits of this page

The three labs are informal demonstrations. They differ from the original procedures (for example, the original three-letter experiment used spoken items, counting aloud and a time limit on answers), each runs only a few trials, and the results say nothing about your memory compared with anyone else's. Where the page gives a number from a study, it is the figure reported in that study or, where marked approximate, the figure as it is commonly reported from the study's graphs.
